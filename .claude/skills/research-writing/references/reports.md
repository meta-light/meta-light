# Reports

How to outline and write research reports and flash notes. For voice and formatting see `style.md`. For thesis, evidence and risk logic see `argumentation.md`.

## What every piece answers

Pick the questions that matter for the subject and make sure the piece answers them:

- What is the opportunity?
- How big is it?
- What is the market or competitive impact?
- How will value flow change, and who captures it?

Define unfamiliar terms early so the reader isn't lost by paragraph three.

## Formats

### Flash note (up to 2,000 words)

For event-driven or market-moving news, earnings reactions, and quick updates to existing coverage.

1. **Top Idea**: three to five sentences. The view, the evidence, what to watch.
2. **Introduction**
3. **Two or three analysis sections**
4. **Research View**: risks and outlook, ending on a clear stance.
5. **Disclaimer**

### Research report (2,000 to 6,000 words)

For deep dives, initiations of coverage and protocol overviews.

1. **Key Takeaways**: four to six bullets.
2. **Introduction**: 300 to 500 words.
3. **Two to four analysis sections**: 400 to 800 words each.
4. **Valuation**: 400 to 700 words. Skip for sponsored and thought leadership pieces.
5. **Risks**: 300 to 500 words.
6. **Conclusion**: 150 to 300 words, with a stance (Long, Short, Overweight, Underweight or Neutral).
7. **Disclaimer**

### Sponsored report

Same structure as a research report, with these changes:

- Sector first, sponsor second. The piece explains the category; the sponsor gets roughly 250 words as one participant in it.
- No valuation, no price target, no investment stance.
- Name the sponsor at the top and use the sponsored disclaimer.

### Thought leadership

Original framework, hypothesis test or primary data study. Same structure as a research report minus Valuation and stance. The "so what" is the framework or finding itself.

## Outline before writing

Write the outline first and check it before drafting. A good outline makes the draft mostly mechanical.

### 1. Why now

Name the catalyst type and the specific trigger:

- **Event-driven**: a launch, upgrade, listing, unlock, regulatory ruling.
- **Market timing**: price dislocation, sentiment extreme, a multiple out of line with peers.
- **Thesis inflection**: a metric just crossed a threshold that changes the story.
- **Emerging narrative**: a category forming that the market hasn't priced.

Then write the thesis in two or three sentences. If you can't, you aren't ready to outline.

### 2. Argument architecture

Three layers. For each, list key points, the fact that supports each one, and any chart you need.

| Layer | Question | Example points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism | What is it and how does it work? | Product design, value accrual path |
| Market | Why does it matter? | Market size, competition, demand drivers |
| Financial proof | How much is it worth? | Revenue, margins, multiples, scenarios |

Every fact from your notes should be allocated to exactly one section. Facts that don't support the thesis get cut.

### 3. Section plan

**Key Takeaways**, typically six bullets: what it is plus the thesis, why now, the key growth metric, valuation, the main risk, and the stance.

**Analysis sections** by report type:

| Report type | Section A | Section B | Section C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Protocol deep dive | Product/mechanism overview | Market opportunity | Business model and value accrual |
| Market structure / comparative | Core business | Competitive comparison | Market performance |
| Thematic / landscape | Technical primer | Market opportunity | Landscape of players |

**Valuation**: methodology, assumptions, bear/base/bull, comps.

**Risks**: only the isolated ones (see the risk integration plan below).

**Conclusion**: refined thesis, one to three catalysts, primary risk, stance and time horizon.

### 4. Comparative frame

Lock the peer set before drafting so comparisons stay consistent across sections. Explain why each peer belongs.

### 5. Risk integration plan

- **Integrated risks** threaten a core assumption. Discuss them in the analysis section where that assumption is introduced.
- **Isolated risks** are tail risks and unknowns that don't drive the model. They go in the Risks section.

### 6. Data requirements

List every number you still need, where you'll get it, and what you'll do if you can't.

### 7. Outline checklist

- [ ] Thesis is falsifiable and names what the market is missing.
- [ ] Catalyst is specific, with timing if known.
- [ ] All three argument layers are filled.
- [ ] Every fact is allocated to a section.
- [ ] Section word targets sum to the format's range.

## Writing each section

### Key Takeaways

- Four to six bullets, each standalone and quantified.
- Relative framing on every metric.
- Include the stance and the main risk.
- Two to four source links across the bullets.

Useful bullet shapes:

- "[Subject] is [mechanism] that [differentiator], with [metric] (+[change] [period])."
- "At [valuation], [token] trades at [multiple] versus [comp] at [multiple]."
- "[Risk] remains the swing factor: if [condition], [consequence]."
- "We are [constructive/cautious/neutral] on [token] over [horizon], contingent on [variable]."

### Introduction

Two to four paragraphs, 300 to 500 words.

- P1: what it is, in plain terms, with one anchoring number.
- P2: why now. Product launch, market timing, regulatory change, competitive shift, or a data inflection.
- P3 to P4: what the report covers and the shape of the argument.

Don't open with history, a rhetorical question or an anecdote.

### Analysis sections

Every paragraph follows mechanism, then data, then implication:

> The vault hedges spot collateral with short perps across four venues [mechanism]. Supply sits at ~$5B against ~$2.5B in spot holdings [data]. That leaves room to roughly double supply before hitting open interest limits on those venues [implication].

- Open each section with an assertion, not a description.
- Every claim gets data. Every number gets context.
- Tie each section back to the thesis.
- Don't end a section by summarizing it.
- Three to six source links per section for the core evidence.

Sub-section shapes:

- **Product component**: one sentence on what it does, two or three on the mechanism, current metrics with context, implication.
- **Competitive comparison**: a table of the metrics that matter, what the table shows, what it means for positioning.
- **Market sizing**: current state with numbers, growth trajectory with evidence, the subject's addressable share, key assumptions.

### Valuation

1. **Methodology**: the approach (P/S, P/E, FDV/fees, DCF, sum of the parts) and why it fits.
2. **Assumptions table**: the key drivers under bear, base and bull, plus time horizon.
3. **Scenarios**: for each case, the conditions, the resulting revenue or metric, the multiple applied, and the implied value.
4. **Comps table**: peers, their revenue, their multiple, a note on why they're comparable.

Credibility habits:

- Anchor conservatively: apply a multiple below the peer median and say why.
- Show sensitivity on the one or two inputs that move the answer most.
- If you probability-weight the cases, show the weights.
- Prefer ranges to false precision.
- Never "could be worth $X" without the math.

### Risks

Three to five risks, ordered by impact: thesis-critical first, then material, then ones to monitor. For each:

1. What the risk is, specific to this subject.
2. Why it matters here.
3. The causal chain: if X, then Y, quantified where possible.
4. Any mitigant, stated as partial, not as a dismissal.

Common categories: regulatory, market, execution, technical, token and governance, counterparty, concentration, liquidity.

"Smart contract risk exists" is not a risk. "The vault's withdrawal path depends on a single upgradeable proxy controlled by a 3-of-5 multisig" is.

### Conclusion

Not a summary. Restate the thesis as the analysis refined it, name one to three catalysts with timing, connect to the primary risk, and state the stance with a horizon and condition.

> We remain constructive on [token] at current levels ([multiple]), with 12-month conviction contingent on [specific metric] through [date].

Stance language:

| Stance | Pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Constructive | "We remain constructive on...", "We view [subject] favorably..." |
| Cautious | "We maintain a cautious stance...", "We see limited near-term upside..." |
| Neutral | "We remain neutral pending...", "Risk/reward looks balanced..." |

Don't end with a question, a call for "more research", or new information.

## Tech investment concepts

Lenses that often explain why something will or won't accrue value. Use the ones that apply.

- **Network effects**: does each new user make the product better for others? Positive or negative, inclusive or exclusive.
- **Friction**: what does it cost a user to start, and to leave?
- **Platform strategy**: does it let others build on it and capture value from that?
- **Come for the tool, stay for the network**: single-player utility that seeds a multi-player network.
- **Scale economics**: do unit costs fall as volume grows?
- **Switching costs**: integrations, liquidity, habits, data.
- **Recurring revenue**: how much is repeatable versus one-off or incentive-driven?
- **Moats**: data, regulatory, ecosystem lock-in, liquidity depth.
- **Marketplace dynamics**: which side is scarce, who sets price, take rate stability.
- **Tech differentiation**: is it defensible or easily forked?
- **Engagement**: active users and retention cohorts, not just signups.
- **Brand**: does it lower acquisition cost?
- **Customer concentration**: how much depends on a few users, integrators or market makers?
- **Gross margin**: what's left after paying LPs, validators, incentives and infrastructure?
