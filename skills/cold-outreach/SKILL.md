# Cold outreach

Source: https://x.com/adriannalakatos/status/2042490448766525532

A cold message gets a reply when it is **short, specific, personal and easy to say yes to**. It doesn't need connections or credentials. The author used cold messages to get a flight to meet the founder of Buildspace, $20k of extra scholarship money, and handwritten lyrics from a band's old manager. Most people never ask. The worst outcome of asking is being ignored, which is where they started.

If the user has a mutual contact who could introduce them, a warm intro usually beats a cold message: use **intro-requests** instead.

## The template

The author reports a reply rate above 40% with this structure:

```
Subject: [the specific thing they posted, did or said]

Hey [first name],

[One sentence proving you paid attention: their post, talk, product or words.]

[One sentence on why you're reaching out, framed around them, not you.]

[One sentence on what you're offering, as an outcome.]

[A low-effort ask: "mind if I send a 2-min demo?" / "got 5 min for a quick yes/no?"]

[Your first name]
```

Five lines: hook, why it matters to them, the offer, the ask, the sign-off. If it can't be said in five lines, the user doesn't understand their offer well enough yet. Help them sharpen it before you write.

## The eight mistakes

| # | Mistake | Don't | Do |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A generic subject line.** If it fails, nothing else gets read. | "Quick question", "Following up", "Partnership opportunity" | Specific enough that only this person could have received it. |
| 2 | **A copy-pasted opener.** You have one line to make them care. | "Hope you're doing well" | "Saw your post on [x], had to reach out." / "I'll keep this short bc you don't care yet." |
| 3 | **Too long.** A wall of text is a wall between you and a reply. | Paragraphs of background | The five-line template |
| 4 | **An ask that feels like homework.** | "Would love to connect sometime" | "Mind if I send a 2-min demo?" / "Got 5 min for a quick yes/no?" |
| 5 | **No follow-up.** Most replies come from follow-ups. No reply doesn't mean no. | Sending once and giving up | The follow-up schedule below |
| 6 | **About you, not them.** | "Let me know if there's anything you need help with" | "This might save your team 5 hours a week." |
| 7 | **Too formal.** It's a vibe check, not a thesis. | Stiff, applicant tone | A smart friend's tone: contractions, fragments, first name only |
| 8 | **Same message on every platform.** | One template everywhere | See "Match the channel" below |

## Match the channel

| Channel | Style |
|---|---|
| X DM | Short, casual, straight to the point. Often no greeting and no sign-off. |
| Cold email | The five-line structure, still direct. |
| Investor email | Lead with traction (users, revenue, growth), no fluff. |
| Institution (university, company department, support) | Direct and specific: your situation, and exactly what you're asking for. |

The rules are the same everywhere; only the energy changes.

## Follow-ups

- **Day 3:** "Bumping this up in case it got buried, no pressure either way."
- **Day 7:** "Worth a quick look or should I close this out?" Giving an easy out often gets the reply. Don't use it on the same person repeatedly.
- **Better than a bump: news.** If there's a real update, send it as the follow-up. For a founder writing to an investor: "Following up from last week. We just hit 500 users and crossed $10k MRR. Would love to revisit the conversation." Traction is the best follow-up.
- **A long-shot ask can take years.** The author followed up about 15 times over four years to get the lyrics. Space follow-ups out (weeks or months after the first two), give each one something new, and stop if the person says no or asks you to stop.

## Workflow

### Writing a message

1. **Collect** before drafting. Ask the user for whatever is missing:
   - who the recipient is and the channel;
   - one real, specific thing about them: a post, talk, product or quote, with a link if possible;
   - what the user offers and the outcome for the recipient;
   - the single ask;
   - any proof, such as traction, a result or a demo link.
2. **Write the subject line** around the specific thing. Offer two or three options.
3. **Draft the five lines.** Check every sentence: does it serve the recipient or the sender? Rewrite sender-centred lines as outcomes for them.
4. **Make the ask a yes/no** that costs under five minutes.
5. **Adapt** to the channel.
6. **Draft the day-3 and day-7 follow-ups** at the same time, plus a news-based version if the user expects an update.

### Critiquing a draft

Go through the eight mistakes in order. For each failure, quote the line, name the mistake and give the rewrite. Then give the full revised message. Fix the subject line, first line and ask first; they decide whether there's a reply.

### Running a campaign

The real secret is volume and iteration. Send many messages (the author suggests 100), track which subject lines, openers and asks get replies, drop what doesn't work and double down on what does. The first 50 will be weak; that's normal. If the user is sending in volume, suggest a simple log: recipient, date, subject, opener variant, ask, follow-ups sent, reply (y/n).

## Guardrails

- **Personalization must be real.** Never invent a post, talk or shared interest the recipient didn't produce. If the user hasn't given one, ask, or leave a `[specific thing they said or did]` placeholder. Fake personalization is worse than none.
- **Never invent proof.** Traction, results and numbers come from the user only; use `[metric]` placeholders otherwise.
- **The 40% reply rate is the author's claim**, not a promise. Don't quote it to the user's recipients or present it as a benchmark.
- **Respect a no.** A clear no or a request to stop ends the follow-ups. Persistence applies to silence, not refusal.
- **Bulk sending.** For commercial email in volume, remind the user of the basics of anti-spam law (e.g. CAN-SPAM, GDPR): honest subject lines, a real identity, and an easy way to opt out.
- **Fragments and casual tone are allowed here** (mistake 7), even though **stop-slop** flags fragments in prose. Keep it human, not gimmicky. Use **storytelling** only if a one-line story helps the hook; a cold email is not the place for a story.

## Output

- **New message:** two or three subject lines, the message, then the day-3 and day-7 follow-ups, then any placeholders the user must fill.
- **Critique:** the failed mistakes, each with the quoted line and the rewrite, then the revised message.
