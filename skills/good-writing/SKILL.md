# Good writing

Source: https://paulgraham.com/goodwriting.html

Writing can be good in two senses: it sounds good, and the ideas are right. Right means more than true: the conclusions that matter most are drawn, and each is explored to the right depth. The two senses are connected. **When a sentence sounds wrong, the idea behind it is often wrong too.** Fixing the sound tends to fix the idea.

This applies to writing used to **develop** ideas: essays, theses, memos, argued research. It applies much less to writing that reports ideas that live elsewhere (an experiment write-up, docs, a textbook), where the writing can be bad while the ideas are good. For those, clarity edits are enough. Don't hunt for idea bugs there.

## Why it works

- **Shaking the bin.** Any constraint on a passage (make it a line shorter, remove a repeated word) forces a rewrite. An honest writer never rewrites a passage into something less true, so any change to the idea has to be an improvement, the way shaking a bin only ever packs things tighter.
- **The writer is the first reader.** Prose that flows is easy to reread, and the easier it is to reread, the easier it is to notice where something catches.
- **Rhythm follows thought.** An essay is a cleaned-up train of thought, and a train of thought has a natural rhythm: simple ideas get short, plain statements; subtle ones need longer sentences to tease out the implications. When the rhythm is wrong, the thought usually is too.

## Revision loop

1. **Reread for catches.** Read the draft as a reader, passage by passage, asking: does anything catch? Does anything feel wrong? Mark every catch, even when you can't yet say why. Graham rereads parts 50–100 times.
2. **At each catch, ask "what do I mean here?"** before you touch the wording. Write the claim in one plain sentence. Often the claim is vague, overstated, missing a step, or not what the paragraph argues. Fix the idea, then rewrite the sentence around it.
3. **Treat repetition as a symptom.** A phrase repeated across nearby paragraphs often means two paragraphs are making the same point, or one point hasn't been worked out. Decide what each paragraph is for before rewording.
4. **Shake it.** For a weak passage, make it one line shorter (or longer) without dropping anything true. Keep the version you like better, which is usually the new one. Keep the constraint small: a huge or arbitrary one (like word-length rules) makes the ideas worse.
5. **Match the rhythm to the idea.** Short, plain sentences for simple claims, longer ones for the subtle parts. Flag regular rhythm (every sentence the same length or shape) as a sign that the form is driving the ideas instead of the other way round.
6. **Fix inserted points.** A point added later into the middle of a draft often breaks the flow, because ideas are tree-shaped and an essay is linear. Restructure so the point grows out of what precedes it. If it doesn't fit anywhere, move it to an endnote or cut it rather than leave a seam.
7. **Check the premises.** Smooth, internally consistent writing can still be false if a premise is false ("if the number of jobs were fixed, immigrants would be taking them"). Good sound only shows internal consistency. List the load-bearing premises and check each against the world.

## Using it as a critic

- **Clumsy writing usually means wrong ideas.** Treat clumsy passages as the likeliest places for reasoning errors, and read them first.
- **Polished writing is not evidence of truth.** For a smooth argument, go straight to step 7: what does it assume, and is that true?
- Point to the specific sentence that catches, then give the idea problem behind it, then the rewrite. "This doesn't flow" alone isn't feedback.

## Relation to other writing skills

Use **stop-slop** to strip AI writing patterns and **research-writing** for house style and report structure. This skill comes before both: it finds where the thinking is wrong. Don't polish a passage whose idea hasn't been fixed yet.

## Output

When revising or critiquing a draft:

1. The catches, in order. For each: the quoted sentence, the idea problem behind it in one line (vague, overstated, missing a step, duplicated, inserted, false premise), and the rewrite.
2. The load-bearing premises, and any that look false or unsupported.
3. Structural notes, if any (a point to move, merge, cut or send to an endnote).
4. If the user asked for a full rewrite, the revised text after the notes. Keep the author's voice and claims; change a claim only where it was wrong, and say where.
